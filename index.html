(Potter County Was Made By the Hand of God, But the Devil Made) Three Mile Island

Al Shade & Jean Romaine Potter County PA, 1979

Al Shade wrote "Potter County Was Made by the Hand of God" after hunting trips to the northern tier, and audiences knew him for it. When the reactor failed he hung the accident onto the end of his own hymn to the place. That is why the title runs to fourteen words.

More

Shade got the idea while shaving. Jean Romaine sang harmony to his melody for sixty years. She joined the Al Shade Show in 1953 as one of the Gesford Sisters, married him in 1955, and performed beside him until her death in 2017.

David Brinkley's newscast carried the song nationally that year. Shade kept the radio show going past its seventieth anniversary in 2022, one of the longest runs in the medium. He died in January 2026, aged 98.

Sources: LebTown profile York Daily Record

Radiation Funk

Maxwell Columbia PA, 1979

Maxwell played funk in the river towns downstream from the plant. Of the six hundred songs the accident produced, theirs is the one central Pennsylvania actually heard on the radio.

More

The band pressed it themselves. Marty Moore wrote it. Moore produced it with Jimmy Harris and Jeff Aston, and the three of them stamped the label with their own company name, TMI Productions. "A New Star" went on the B side, and the band counts both sides as local hits.

They still play, now as The Maxwell Project.

Roulette

Bruce Springsteen NJ, 1979

Springsteen wrote this within days of the accident. He cut it at the Power Station in New York in the first week of April 1979, while crews were still bringing the reactor under control. It was the first song he recorded for The River.

More

Then he left it off the album. The tape sat for nine years. Columbia finally issued it in 1988 on the B side of "One Step Up", a strange end for the song that opened the sessions. Springsteen put it on Tracks in 1998 and has played it live 57 times since, starting on the 1988 tour.

Tom lists it twice, and he is right to. The acoustic demo sits under 1979 and the band version under 1988.

Sources: Discogs: One Step Up, 1988 Brucebase

London Calling

The Clash UK, 1979

Joe Strummer got Three Mile Island into a single line. He sings "a nuclear error, but I have no fear", and that error is the reactor, which failed in March while the Clash were touring America for the first time.

More

Strummer packed the verse with everything he had been reading that year. He took "London is drowning and I live by the river" from a real fear that the Thames would flood the city. Engineers answered that one three years later with the Thames Barrier.

The band recorded it that autumn and released the single on 7 December 1979, nine months after the accident it mentions in passing.

Sources: Wikipedia: London Calling

Harrisburg / Your Money and Your Life / Tell ‘Em No

The Fourth Wall NYC, 1980

For her 1980 documentary We Are the Guinea Pigs, Joan Harvey filmed families on the farms and in the kitchens around Harrisburg and let them describe the accident in their own words. Between interviews, a band plays topical songs that parse the families' reactions in musical form.

More

The film credits no performers beyond members of the 4th Wall Repertory Company, the house band of a political theater collective on East 4th Street. Eric Grunin, the band's bassist, keeps the group's archive online and names the players: John Amato and Mike Fogarty on guitars, Grunin on bass, Joe Hudson on drums. Amato wrote or co-wrote five of the six songs, and Jocko Marcellino of Sha Na Na wrote Harrisburg. The singer in the photo is almost certainly Amato, filmed at Halifax, PA on August 12, 1979.

One Kind Favor cut the soundtrack onto an eight inch lathe, pressed 27 copies, and gave the download away. The reissue holds six songs where this list had three. War Machine, We Are The Guinea Pigs and The Crop Is In arrive here for the first time. You can watch the film free on archive.org.

Tsjerno Harris

RTC (Rocking Teenage Combo) Netherlands, 1986

Stichting Laka, the Dutch anti-nuclear documentation centre, posted this audio in April 2026. That is why no search had ever turned it up. The song was missing from this list until today.

More

Two Dutch acts shared a seven inch called Is It Safe. They cut it in the months after April 1986, pressed 250 copies, and slipped an A3 sheet of newspaper cuttings about Chernobyl and radiation into each sleeve. RTC take the A side at 45 rpm. De Fabriek take the B side with Harrisnobyl, exactly ten minutes at 33 and a third.

Both titles fold Tsjernobyl into Harrisburg. By 1986 the two accidents had merged into one idea. Laka hosts the A side only, so De Fabriek's half is still missing.

Corrections to the blog listing

  1. "No Nuke Blues" - Vic Sadot & the Crazy Planet Band: The blog credits 'Vic Sadot & the Crazy Planet Band.' The earliest documented release of the song is the 1988 LP Ride the Wind, credited to The Crazy Planet Band (Crazy Planet Productions, catalogue CPP-9088, US). The recording linked here is a later acoustic re-recording with an added 2011 Fukushima verse, officially released in 2016 on the album Truth Troubadour; that 1988 LP cut and the original 1979 composition do not appear to be online anywhere.
  2. "Don't Melt Down on My Baby / Nuclear Waste / Plutonium" - Tru Fax & the Insaniacs: The blog's 1979 entry bundles three songs under one line, but only two, "Don't Meltdown on My Baby" and "Nuclear Waste," have any surviving audio, and that audio is a 2014 re-recording rather than a 1979 original. The third song named in the same line, "Plutonium," has no known recording; per Mariotte it was played live only a couple of times, bombed at CBGB in October 1979, and has rarely been performed since.
  3. "Niagra Baby" - Rejex: The blog spells the title "Niagra Baby." Both the record label and Discogs spell it "Niagara Baby."
  4. "Ain't Nowhere You Can Run" - Holly Near: The title on the official releases is "Ain't No Where You Can Run" / "Ain't Nowhere To Run," slightly different word spacing than the blog's "Ain't Nowhere You Can Run."
  5. "Radiation" - T.C. Radation (sic): The blog lists this as title 'Radiation' by artist 'T.C. Radation (sic)'. The record itself has it the other way around: the artist is Radation and the song title is 'T.C. Radation' (rendered as 'T.C. Radiation' on the digital reissue). There is no separate artist called T.C. Radation.
  6. "Critical Mass" - Little Kenny & His Band of Renown: The blog credits 'Little Kenny & His Band of Renown'. The actual credit, per Discogs and the CDBaby reissue, is Little Kenny Wilson And The Band Of Renown (Kenny A. Wilson). A different, unrelated 1975 Little Kenny Wilson And His Band Of Renown release (Flying Together And Other Short Subjects, Hunky Dory Organization) exists on Discogs and was checked; it has no Critical Mass track and is not this record.
  7. "Melt Down" - Hans Edler: The blog lists this song twice, once as Hans Edler and once as Hans Elder. Both are the same 1979 Swedish recording by Hans Edler.
  8. "Melt Down" - Hans Elder: The blog spells the artist 'Hans Elder' with no location; the correct spelling is Hans Edler (Sweden). The same blog page separately lists 'Melt Down - Hans Edler (Sweden)' a few lines earlier with the correct spelling, so this entry is very likely a duplicate transcription of that same 45 rather than a second artist.
